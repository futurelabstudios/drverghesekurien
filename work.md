# Work

Kurien’s working life, as Amul tells it, had four long chairs: executive head of the Kaira union from 1950; founder chairman of the National Dairy Development Board from 1965 to 1998; chairman of the Gujarat Co-operative Milk Marketing Federation from 1973 to 2006; and chairman of the Institute of Rural Management Anand from 1979 to 2006.

The idea he repeated was that farmers should hold the technology and the professional management, and through them a larger share of the consumer’s rupee. A *Business Line* profile quotes him, from an interview around 2004, on why a brand mattered: without one, the farmer remains a contract supplier, and the value sits in marketing.

Source: [Amul Dairy](https://www.amuldairy.com/dr_kurien.php); [*The Hindu Business Line*, 9 September 2012](https://www.thehindubusinessline.com/opinion/The-man-who-empowered-through-milk/article20499872.ece).

## Before he arrived: Polson and the union

In late 1946, Tribhuvandas Kishibhai Patel organised farmers in Kheda district who were being squeezed by Polson, the dairy of Pestonji Edulji Dalal, then the main buyer. The cooperative had milk and a market in Bombay, and it did not have sound processing. Milk could sour on the way. Patel leased part of the government creamery where Kurien was posted. The machinery broke down. Kurien repaired it in his spare time.

The *Business Line* account of that partnership says Kurien told Patel the lasting fix was a plate pasteuriser, at a cost he put around Rs 60,000. Patel said he would raise the money if Kurien would install it and run it. That was the bargain that kept the engineer in Anand. By the end of 1948 the Kheda cooperative was collecting on the order of 5,000 litres a day from about 430 farmers.

Amul’s 2012 note says the first dairy cooperative union in Gujarat was formed in 1946 with two village societies. Harish Damodaran, writing in *The Indian Express* in 2024, records that the Ajarpura society — union code 0001 — was registered on 7 August 1947. The Ramon Magsaysay citation of 1963 describes the union as organised in 1948 from two village societies and a dairy then processing 500 pounds of milk a day. The dates name different steps: the decision to organise, the registration of the first village society, and the union the award committee was looking at fifteen years later.

By 1962, the Magsaysay record says, the union had 219 societies and 46,400 members, and the year’s milk had grossed over 6 million US dollars. Bombay State contracted for the pasteurised milk. The new plant, helped by the Bombay government, UNICEF, New Zealand under the Colombo Plan, and others, was described by the award foundation as the first in India to make milk powder, condensed milk, and a special powder for babies, and the first in the world to powder buffalo milk. The trade name was Amul.

Damodaran writes that Amul is not, despite a common belief, an abbreviation of “Anand Milk Union Limited.” This archive did not find a matching brand-origin page on Amul’s own site during this research, so the point is given as his, not as a house definition.

## Dalaya and the buffalo

Kurien did not claim the powdering of buffalo milk as his own laboratory victory. Tushaar Shah, who worked in his office during the planning of Operation Flood II, remembers him saying that in the Amul years H. M. Dalaya did the work and Kurien did the talking. Nirmala Kurien called the three of them — her father, Tribhuvandas Patel, and “Dalaya uncle” — the trinity of Amul. A family photograph of the three men is in [the photo credits](media/photos/CREDITS.md).

The technical claim itself is in the Magsaysay citation: the plant was the first in the world to convert buffalo milk into powder. Buffalo milk was what Kheda had. Cow milk was not.

## Operation Flood

The national programme began from a prime minister’s visit, told in [Stories](stories/anecdotes.md). Lal Bahadur Shastri stayed overnight in Ajarpura on 30 October 1964, inaugurated the Kaira union’s cattle-feed plant at Kanjari the next day, and on 2 December 1964 wrote to the chief ministers proposing a national federation that would carry “the spirit of Anand” across India. NDDB was established in September 1965 with Kurien as chairman.

Operation Flood, drawn up in the late 1960s and launched in 1970, used gifted dairy commodities — skim milk powder and butter oil, largely from the European Economic Community, through the World Food Programme — as a fund. The commodities were sold, and the money built cooperatives that would procure, process, and market milk. Amul describes an investment of about Rs 1,700 crore over 25 years.

The Food and Agriculture Organization’s account of the design splits the work into phases:

- Phase I, from 1970, under an agreement with the World Food Programme for 126,000 tonnes of skim milk powder and 42,000 tonnes of butter oil. Village cooperatives in the best milksheds were linked to Bombay, Calcutta, Delhi, and Madras.
- Phase II, which that FAO page places in 1981–85, brought in about 150 million US dollars from the World Bank plus further EEC commodity aid, and folded in other dairy projects.
- Phase III aimed at making the cooperatives self-sustaining, with about 360 million US dollars from the World Bank, further European help, and NDDB’s own money.

The World Food Prize dates the whole arc as 1970 to 1996, and dates its second phase from 1979. The two public chronologies do not match to the year. What they share is the shape: cities first, then a wider grid, then institutions that were supposed to stand without aid.

When Kurien retired as NDDB chairman in 1998, the prize biography says the programmes involved more than 10 million producers in 81,000 cooperatives, supplying almost five million tonnes of milk a year to over 1,000 towns. An opening summary on the same page speaks of 86,000 cooperatives and nearly 250 million people with better access to dairy foods. At the start, in 1970, the same biography says procurement was 190,000 tonnes from 278,000 farmers. India became the world’s largest milk producer in 1998, passing the United States.

Amul’s later snapshot, from its own pages, is larger still, because it counts years after 1998: on the order of 14 million farmers, more than 100,000 villages, nearly 180 unions, 24 states. Its 2012 note put national milk production at 20 million metric tonnes a year in the 1960s and 122 million in 2011, and Gujarat’s Amul system at 16,100 societies and 3.2 million members. Figures in [Impact](impact.md) are always tied to the year of the source.

NDDB’s corporate film, linked in [Videos](media/videos.md), tells the same commodity logic in the board’s own words.

## The Anand pattern

The pattern, as the FAO page and Kurien’s colleagues describe it, is a three-tier cooperative. Farmers own a village society that tests and buys milk twice a day. Societies own a district union that processes. Unions own a marketing federation. Professionals are hired by the farmers’ boards. A powder plant carries the flush season, when buffaloes and cows give more than the liquid market will take, over into the lean months.

Shah remembers the 1977 version in one sentence Kurien offered a room of young officers: there are many ways to skin a cat, and at NDDB there was one, the Amul pattern. He wanted government milk schemes and multinational dairies kept out of that design.

Amul, citing the National Council of Applied Economic Research, says that in Operation Flood areas more than 75 per cent of rural milch-animal households were small and marginal farmers, with under two hectares, or were landless, and that nearly 70 per cent of village dairy cooperative members came from households with one or two milch animals.

## Beyond milk

Amul’s account says the government then asked him to carry the same umbrella to other crops.

- In 1979 the Oilseeds Growers’ Cooperative Project set out to link growers and consumers, steady prices, and cut imports. Kurien put a brand on that trade: Dhara.
- In the mid-1980s, at the government’s request, a pilot bought fruits and vegetables for Delhi from growers in several states.
- The cooperative idea was also tried on trees and on salt. M. S. Sriram, reviewing Kurien’s autobiography in 2006, writes that the book treats the salt experiment as the one clear failure among those later ventures. This archive did not read the book, so the sentence stays as Sriram’s reading.

In 1979 he founded IRMA so that rural cooperatives would have managers trained for them, rather than borrowed from the city and left unprepared. The institute’s own page says the Amul story was what convinced him that professional management could serve people the market had ignored. In 1988 he helped reshape the National Cooperative Dairy Federation of India as a national body of the state dairy federations, and he set up the Anandalaya Education Society for the children of people working in the Anand institutions. In 1994 he helped set up Vidya Dairy, a working dairy where dairy-technology students could train on a modern line.

The World Food Prize notes that he also chaired the National Cooperative Dairy Federation of India, served as chancellor of the University of Allahabad, and sat on other boards. It does not give the years for the Allahabad chancellorship.

## How he left the chairs

He left NDDB in 1998. Amrita Patel, the veterinarian he had brought up inside the institutions, succeeded him. In 2006 the board of GCMMF moved against him. *Frontline* reported that a no-confidence motion was to be put on 24 March, and that on 20 March he resigned, after 33 years as chairman. He said the decision also deferred to a Gujarat High Court order on co-opted members of cooperative boards. *The Economic Times* reported the announcement in a full IRMA auditorium on 20 March 2006. He was 84.
