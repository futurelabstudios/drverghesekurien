## The problem Kurien wanted to solve

By the late 1970s Operation Flood was building thousands of village cooperatives and district unions. They needed managers — people who could run a dairy plant, a balance sheet and a farmers' meeting — and who would **work for farmers** rather than for shareholders.

India's top business schools were not supplying them. Kurien observed that many graduates of IIM Ahmedabad went abroad or to multinationals and banks. In his framing, an MNC manager serves a handful of distant shareholders, while an Amul manager serves around **400,000 farmers**.

He also had a sharper belief, recorded by Katar Singh: **"rural" does not mean "backward."** Rural managers should be competent professionals, paid fairly — he wanted IRMA graduates paid comparably to IIM graduates and did not want them living in thatched huts to prove their commitment.

Underlying it all was his reading of the economy: around 70% of Indians lived in rural areas (plus about 4% urban poor) but earned a small share of national income. Organising them into institutions they owned, run by professionals, was the lever.

## The IIM Ahmedabad origin story

The best-known origin story — told by IRMA itself and reported in several places:

- In the late 1970s Kurien sat on the **Board of Governors of IIM Ahmedabad**, invited by **Ravi J. Matthai**, IIM-A's founding full-time director (*Business Today* describes Matthai as Kurien's cousin; ⚠️ we'd like a second source).
- Kurien argued that IIM-A should train managers for agriculture and rural India, and suggested graduates serve India for some years before going abroad.
- A prominent industrialist on the board mocked the idea, reportedly with a jibe involving cigars.
- Kurien's reported reply: **"No, you continue to teach them how to suck on cigars."**
- He never attended another IIM-A board meeting. Matthai encouraged him to build his own institution.

🗣️ This story is retold by IRMA's own communications team (*The Logical Indian*, 2019) and by *Business Today* (2000). **If you heard Kurien tell it himself, please add your version.**

## Founding: 14 December 1979

IRMA was registered and began on **14 December 1979** — the date still celebrated as **Foundation Day** (the 40th was marked on 14 December 2019).

It began as a centre for management and consultancy for rural development within the **second phase of Operation Flood**. Under Kurien it quickly widened its mandate: not just dairy, but all rural producers' organisations — cooperatives in oilseeds, fisheries, forestry, handlooms, credit and more, along with NGOs.

**Same year, same town:** 1979 is also the year Kurien launched the Oilseeds Growers' Cooperative Project. IRMA was conceived as the human-resource engine for this expanding cooperative universe.

## Getting government approval — the Indira Gandhi gambit

As told by Katar Singh, who was drafting the institute's replies at the time:

- IRMA's proposal sat with the **Ministry of Agriculture for more than two years**. The Expenditure Finance Committee kept sending small queries.
- Kurien invited **Prime Minister Indira Gandhi** to be chief guest at IRMA's **first convocation**.
- She accepted. The Ministry, realising the PM was coming to an institute it had not approved, **cleared the proposal quickly**.

## Building the campus "brick by brick"

- Architect: **Achyut Kanvinde**, one of India's leading modernist architects (he also designed NDDB buildings in Anand). The campus covers about **60 acres** in Anand.
- Kurien **visited the construction site every week** (Tushaar Shah, 2017 convocation speech).
- When **Morarji Desai** questioned why so much effort was going into the campus, Kurien replied that IRMA aimed to produce **"kings"** — and you cannot produce kings in a **pig-sty**. Shah explains that "kings" meant people who would think and dream big.
- Kurien later said everything at IRMA was built "brick by brick."

### Translating Kurien

From 1980, **Tushaar Shah** translated Kurien's English speeches into Gujarati for farmers. Kurien rehearsed with him and asked him to match his **tone and pauses**. At a 1984 dairy plant inauguration in Sabarkantha, Kurien asked that farmers be described as **"karmachaari"** (people who employ/work) rather than **"sevak"** (servants) — a small word choice that captured who was meant to be in charge.

## What IRMA taught: the curriculum

IRMA effectively **created "rural management" as a discipline** — combining knowledge of rural society with modern management.

**Programme in Rural Management (PRM)** — the flagship, later called PGDRM and now PGDM(RM):

- About **88 weeks** long (as of 2000).
- Around **40%** experience-based learning: fieldwork, summer training and management traineeship.
- Described by its early planners as the **world's first management programme with extended fieldwork and organisational training**, blending social sciences with core management.
- **100% attendance** policy (reported 2019).

**Fellow Programme in Rural Management** — doctoral-level, to build researchers and teachers in the field.

**Executive PGDM(RM)** — launched **2019** for mid-career professionals.

**Management Development Programmes (MDPs)** — short courses for cooperative boards, managers and development professionals.

Kurien's challenge to faculty, recalled by Katar Singh: **how many of you have actually managed an organisation?** He believed you could not really teach management without having done it.

## Fieldwork: learning in the village

Fieldwork was IRMA's signature and still is.

- Students spend roughly **8–10 weeks living in a village**, attached to one of hundreds of partner cooperatives and NGOs (about **600** designated organisations around 2000).
- Many host villages lacked toilets and other basics.
- The aim: understand how rural people live, earn and make decisions, before trying to manage anything for them.
- Followed by an organisational training segment and a management traineeship inside a rural organisation.

## Students, admissions and placements

**Admissions (c. 2000):** about **5,000 applicants for 60 seats**. Tests aimed to find *commitment* to rural development as well as ability. Selection today includes a written ability test and personal interview.

**Where graduates went (c. 2000, *Business Today*):**

| Destination | Share |
|---|---|
| GCMMF (Amul) or NDDB | ~30% |
| NGOs, cooperatives and other agencies | ~50% |
| Donor agencies | ~10–15% |
| Left the sector | ~15% per batch |

Of about 1,200 alumni at that time, roughly 60% were still in the sector — a retention rate the then-director considered very high.

By **2019**, about **3,000 alumni**, with over **60%** in rural and allied organisations (NDDB chairman's speech, 40th Foundation Day).

**The salary question.** Kurien wanted IRMA graduates paid like IIM graduates. In practice, rural-sector salaries were lower; deputy director K. Prathap Reddy said in 2000 that IRMA served clients who needed talent but could not pay open-market rates.

## Money: the corpus and the cost of a mission

From *Business Today* (2000):

- **NDDB provided the initial corpus** in two tranches: **₹9 crore and ₹8 crore**. It had grown to **₹33 crore**.
- Interest yielded about **₹4 crore a year**, but this stagnated as interest rates fell.
- Consultancy and projects brought in about **₹2.5 crore a year**.
- Each student was subsidised by **₹1–1.5 lakh** over two years.
- Expenditure had caught up with income — the tension of a mission-driven school.

## Kurien as chairman: style, rules, mantras

Katar Singh's list of Kurien's "guru mantras" (paraphrased):

1. Integrity and sincerity above everything.
2. Do more than people expect of you.
3. Attend to small details.
4. Draft, revise and rehearse important letters and calls.
5. Be punctual — and insist others are too.
6. Know what is going on in your organisation.
7. Make time for every employee, including the most junior.
8. Observe courtesy and etiquette with visitors and in official settings.

Other recurring themes:

- **"The India of the future belongs to the young Indians."**
- If one Kurien could transform dairy, thousands of IRMA graduates could transform the rural sector.
- He imagined an **All India Rural Management Service**, modelled on the IAS. Singh notes it was partly realised: IRMA graduates went on to lead Amul, GCMMF and ITC's agri-business division.
- He was sceptical of economists and social scientists who **wrote about development rather than doing it**.
- Critics called him autocratic and ruthless; many staff feared him. Singh found him compassionate and accessible.

## The 2005 crisis

The bitterest chapter in IRMA's history — and one that should be told fairly.

- By 2005 Kurien had chaired IRMA for over **25 years** and the memorandum of association gave him a **lifetime chairmanship**.
- The **Assistant Charity Commissioner** approved IRMA's memorandum *except* the lifetime-chairmanship clause, saying it was not in the institution's interest. An appeal to the Joint Charity Commissioner in Baroda upheld this.
- Some board members, reportedly aligned with NDDB chair **Dr. Amrita Patel**, questioned his continued chairmanship.
- A board meeting set for **1 April 2005** was stayed by a local court; members opposed to Kurien met anyway and formed a committee to find a replacement.
- On **7 April 2005**, Kurien dismissed director **K. Prathap Reddy** and had his office sealed. Kurien called the challenge "a conspiracy by outsiders against me and the institute."
- Government representatives on the board (from the Gujarat and Union rural development departments) signed a resolution calling the dismissal illegal. Kurien said five members' terms had lapsed and moved to co-opt five new members; acting director **L. K. Vaswani** asked those new members to stay away.
- Cases went to the Anand civil court and the **Gujarat High Court** (Reddy's challenge to his termination; a faculty petition on the board).
- **2006** — Kurien's tenure as Founder Chairman ended (Katar Singh dates it "until 2006"). The same year he left GCMMF.

## After Kurien: 2006–2024

- IRMA continued as an autonomous institute, chaired over the years by figures from public life and the dairy sector.
- **2012** — Kurien dies; IRMA institutes the **Dr. Verghese Kurien Memorial Lecture**. IRMA's *Network* magazine publishes a tribute issue.
- **2019** — 40th Foundation Day (14 December); 38th annual convocation (18 April 2019); Executive PGDM(RM) launched; 42nd PGDM(RM) batch around this time.
- **2022** — Union Minister of Cooperation **Amit Shah** is chief guest at a conference at IRMA.
- **Rankings:** 5th overall in *Business Today*'s 2000 survey (1st among sectoral schools); 5th among private B-schools in Times B-School 2019; 20th among private MBA institutions (Outlook 2022); **54th in NIRF 2025 (Management)**.

## 2025: IRMA becomes part of Tribhuvan Sahkari University

- **3 February 2025** — Tribhuvan Sahkari University Bill introduced in the Lok Sabha.
- **26 March 2025** — passed by the Lok Sabha.
- **1 April 2025** — passed by the Rajya Sabha (voice vote).
- The university is named after **Tribhuvandas Kishibhai Patel** — the farmer-leader who persuaded the young Kurien to stay in Anand in 1949.
- It is described as India's **first national university dedicated to the cooperative sector** and is an **institution of national importance**.
- **5 July 2025** — Union Cooperation Minister **Amit Shah** performs the *bhoomi pujan* for a **125-acre** campus in Anand.
- **IRMA is subsumed as one of the university's schools**, with its academic autonomy preserved. The university began operations in 2025, with IRMA's ongoing programme plus new courses.
- One-time capital grant of **₹500 crore** from the Government of India; aims to train lakhs of cooperative-sector workers a year; Pune's **VAMNICOM** is the first affiliated institution.
- Vice-Chancellor (per Wikipedia, 2025): **Dr. S. P. Singh**. Website: tsu.ac.in.

There is a fitting symmetry: the institute Kurien built to professionalise cooperatives now sits inside a university named after the cooperator who kept him in Anand.

## Alumni and impact

Examples of what IRMA graduates and associates have built or led:

- **SIFFS (South Indian Federation of Fishermen Societies)** — founded by an early IRMA graduate who saw Kerala's fishermen losing value to middlemen, and pooled catches through a cooperative.
- Leadership roles at **Amul/GCMMF, NDDB, ITC agri-business**, state rural livelihood missions, MGNREGA programmes, UNICEF, the World Bank, ActionAid and many NGOs.
- Associated public figures listed by Wikipedia include **Sanjay Ghose** (rural development worker), **Raju Narisetti** (founding editor of *Mint*), and others. ⚠️ Please help confirm which were graduates.
